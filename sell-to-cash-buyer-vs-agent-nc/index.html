Compare net proceeds, not list price

A listing price is only the starting point. Agent commissions are negotiable but often total 5% to 6% of the price. Then come pre-listing repairs, buyer inspection requests, concessions, NC excise tax of $1 per $500 of the price, attorney fees, and every month of mortgage, taxes, insurance and utilities while the house is on the market.

Example: a $300,000 house that needs work

Say a house would sell for $300,000 fully updated, but needs $35,000 of repairs. Listing as-is means fewer buyers, and many can't get loans on it. Fixing it first means paying for the work, taking on contractor risk and waiting. A cash offer accounts for those repairs up front, so you skip the work, the commissions and the wait.

When a cash sale makes the most sense

Major repairs, inherited homes you don't want to manage, tenants in place, foreclosure deadlines, divorce, or a move on a fixed date. In these cases, certainty and speed are often worth more than a higher price that may never close.

When listing makes more sense

If your home is updated, in a high-demand area, and you have time, an agent will often get you more. We would rather tell you that than make an offer that doesn't fit.

Frequently asked questions

Will I get less selling to a cash buyer?

Usually the headline price is lower than a fully updated retail listing. But after commissions, repairs, closing costs and months of mortgage, tax and insurance payments, the net difference is often smaller than people expect, and sometimes there is none.

When should I list with an agent instead?

If your home is in good, updated condition, you're not in a hurry and you can handle showings and inspection repairs, listing will often get you a higher price. We'll tell you if we think that's the case.

How do I compare offers fairly?

Compare your net proceeds, not the sale price: price minus commissions, repairs, closing costs, and the costs of holding the house until it sells.
